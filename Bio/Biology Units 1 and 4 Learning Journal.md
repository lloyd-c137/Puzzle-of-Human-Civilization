Organization of natural systems


- **Learn: Earth's natural systems** — marked complete in the course interface.
- **Learn: The biosphere** — marked complete in the course interface.


Living things are organized into nested levels, and each level can be understood as a system whose parts interact.

```text
organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere
```

- An **organism** is one individual living thing.
- A **population** is a group of organisms of the same species living in the same area.
- A **community** is all the populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
- An **ecosystem** includes a community plus the nonliving environment it interacts with.
- A **biome** is a large region defined mainly by climate and its characteristic organisms, such as a desert, grassland, or tropical rainforest.
- The **biosphere** is the global system containing all ecosystems and all places where life exists.

- **Biotic factors** are living components or effects of living things: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, predation, competition, and disease.
- **Abiotic factors** are nonliving physical or chemical conditions: light, temperature, water, air, soil, minerals, pH, and salinity.

- The **atmosphere** is the layer of gases around Earth.
- The **hydrosphere** includes Earth's water.
- The **geosphere** includes Earth's rocks, soil, and solid structure.
- The **biosphere** includes life and the regions where life interacts with the other Earth systems.

These systems are connected. For example, rainfall from the atmosphere enters the hydrosphere and soil in the geosphere; plants in the biosphere use that water and can alter the soil and atmosphere. Matter cycles among systems, while energy—mostly from the Sun—flows through living systems and is eventually released as heat.

A plant in the biosphere grows in soil from the geosphere, takes in water from the hydrosphere, and exchanges gases with the atmosphere. The spheres are different categories, but they work together as one Earth system.

The hydrosphere is Earth's water in all its forms; the geosphere is Earth's solid rock, soil, minerals, and landforms. They interact when water flows over land, erodes rock, enters soil, or becomes groundwater.

A glacier is moving ice, so it belongs to the **hydrosphere**. The ground and rocks it scrapes, erodes, and carries as sediment belong to the **geosphere**. Therefore, the most directly involved spheres are the hydrosphere and geosphere.

An ecosystem is not just a collection of organisms. It is the interaction between living organisms and the nonliving conditions that affect them.

- A **biome（生物群系）** is one large climate-based region containing many related ecosystems.
- The **biosphere（生物圈）** is the whole global system of places on Earth where life exists.
- The biosphere contains all biomes. For example, deserts, grasslands, forests, tundra, and oceans are all parts of the biosphere.

A biome is one major chapter in the story of life on Earth; the biosphere is the entire book.

Abiotic factors influence both **where an organism can be found** and **whether it can survive there**. The geographic area where an organism occurs throughout its lifetime is its **range**.

1. Physical barriers: Mountains, deserts, floods, wildfires, and volcanoes can block movement or separate suitable areas. Aquatic organisms may also be restricted to freshwater or saltwater.
2. Survival conditions: Temperature, light, and moisture affect metabolism, movement, growth, reproduction, and the ability to carry out life processes. A factor can be harmful when there is too little or too much of it.

- Chemical nutrients in soil, including nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium;
- salinity, or the saltiness of water;
- pH, which describes how acidic or basic an environment is;
- dissolved oxygen in water.

Reef-building corals are mainly found in warm tropical and subtropical waters. Water temperature limits their range: they generally cannot survive in water below about 18°C. This illustrates the reasoning pattern:

```text
abiotic factor → effect on survival → geographic range
```

A species is not distributed randomly. Its range reflects the environmental conditions it can tolerate, the barriers it cannot cross, and the conditions that allow it to survive and reproduce.

### Learning record — Lesson 3 article interpretation — An organism's niche

#### question

[https://www.khanacademy.org/science/hs-bio/x230b3ff252126bb6:ecology-and-natural-systems/x230b3ff252126bb6:an-organisms-niche/a/organism-niche?referrer=share_link](https://www.khanacademy.org/science/hs-bio/x230b3ff252126bb6:ecology-and-natural-systems/x230b3ff252126bb6:an-organisms-niche/a/organism-niche?referrer=share_link)

#### respond

[Khan Academy — An organism's niche](https://www.khanacademy.org/science/hs-bio/x230b3ff252126bb6%3Aecology-and-natural-systems/x230b3ff252126bb6%3Aan-organisms-niche/a/organism-niche)

Every organism needs suitable abiotic conditions to develop, survive, and reproduce. Its **tolerance** is its ability to survive variation in an abiotic factor, such as temperature, sunlight, salinity, acidity, or dissolved oxygen. Each factor has a tolerance range:

- Optimal conditions: the organism can survive and reproduce successfully.
- Stressful conditions: the organism may survive, but growth or reproduction is reduced.
- Extreme conditions: conditions pass the limits of tolerance, so the organism cannot survive.

- The **fundamental niche** is the full set of environmental conditions in which an organism could exist, based mainly on its abiotic tolerance ranges.
- The **realized niche** is the portion of that potential space the organism actually uses after competition, predation, and resource availability are considered.

In general, when biologists say “an organism's niche,” they usually mean its realized niche. The niche therefore includes both abiotic requirements and interactions with other organisms.

- A **specialist** has a narrow niche, needs specific resources, and often has a restricted geographic range. The Kirtland's warbler, for example, nests near young jack pine trees.
- A **generalist** has a broad niche, can use many resources, and often lives in many environments. Raccoons are generalists because they can live in forests and cities and eat varied foods.

A niche is more than an address. It describes the conditions an organism can tolerate, the resources it uses, and the relationships that determine where it actually lives.

Caterpillars eat plant leaves, while adult butterflies usually feed on flower nectar. The same species can therefore have different resource use and interactions.

### Learning record — 2026-08-26 — Interactions between populations

#### question

interpret the video for me

#### respond

Khan Academy video: “Interactions between populations.”

Interspecific interactions are relationships between different species. The main interaction types are:

- Competition (-/-): both species are harmed because they use the same limited resource.
- Predation (+/-): one species benefits by consuming another. **Herbivory** is predation in which an animal eats a plant.
- Parasitism (+/-): the parasite benefits while the host is harmed.
- Mutualism (+/+): both species benefit.
- Commensalism (+/0): one species benefits while the other is not noticeably affected.

Symbiosis is the broad category of long-term, close relationships that includes parasitism, mutualism, and commensalism.

A clownfish receives shelter from a sea anemone while helping protect the anemone, which is mutualism. A louse feeding on a human is parasitism. A cheetah eating prey and a goat eating grass are both examples of predation; the goat example is herbivory.

The signs show the effect on each species: **+** benefits, **-** is harmed, and **0** is not noticeably affected.

Predation is not limited to dramatic hunting. Herbivory is also predation because one species consumes another.

#### respond

Khan Academy video “Predator-prey population cycles.”

When prey are abundant and predators are few, predators can increase. More predators then reduce the prey population. When prey become scarce, predators have less food and their population decreases. With fewer predators, the prey population can recover, creating a cycle.

Snowshoe hares are prey and Canadian lynx are predators. Their populations rise and fall in a related pattern, although the predator peak usually follows the prey increase.

The cycle is a feedback loop: more prey supports more predators; more predators reduce prey; less prey reduces predators; fewer predators allow prey to increase.

Predator-prey interaction is only part of the explanation. Abiotic factors, food availability, stress, and other species can also affect both populations.

### Learning record — 2026-08-26 — Interactions within and among species

Khan Academy video “Interactions within and among species.”

**Intraspecific** interactions occur within one species. **Interspecific** interactions occur between different species. As population density rises, competition usually becomes stronger. Resource partitioning lets species reduce competition by using different spaces, resources, or times.

Different species may live in different parts of the same tree, or one species may feed at night while another feeds during the day. If two species use exactly the same niche and limiting resources, the competitive exclusion principle predicts that one may outcompete the other.

Species do not live in isolation. Competition can occur within a species or between species, but sharing a habitat is possible when species divide resources or occupy different niches.

**Intra-** means within the same species; **inter-** means between different species.
