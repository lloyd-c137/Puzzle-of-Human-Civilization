#### Session — 2026-08-21 — Organization of natural systems

**Central idea:** Living things are organized into nested levels, and each level can be understood as a system whose parts interact.

**Levels of organization:**

```text
organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere
```

- An **organism** is one individual living thing.
- A **population** is a group of organisms of the same species living in the same area.
- A **community** is all the populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
- An **ecosystem** includes a community plus the nonliving environment it interacts with.
- A **biome** is a large region defined mainly by climate and its characteristic organisms, such as a desert, grassland, or tropical rainforest.
- The **biosphere** is the global system containing all ecosystems and all places where life exists.

**Biotic and abiotic factors:**

- **Biotic factors** are living components or effects of living things: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, predation, competition, and disease.
- **Abiotic factors** are nonliving physical or chemical conditions: light, temperature, water, air, soil, minerals, pH, and salinity.

**Earth's natural systems:**

- The **atmosphere** is the layer of gases around Earth.
- The **hydrosphere** includes Earth's water.
- The **geosphere** includes Earth's rocks, soil, and solid structure.
- The **biosphere** includes life and the regions where life interacts with the other Earth systems.

These systems are connected. For example, rainfall from the atmosphere enters the hydrosphere and soil in the geosphere; plants in the biosphere use that water and can alter the soil and atmosphere. Matter cycles among systems, while energy—mostly from the Sun—flows through living systems and is eventually released as heat.
#### The four Earth spheres

| Sphere | Chinese | What it includes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atmosphere | 大气层；大气圈 | The gases surrounding Earth | Air, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and clouds |
| Hydrosphere | 水圈 | All of Earth's water, including liquid water, ice, and water vapor | Oceans, rivers, glaciers, groundwater, and clouds |
| Geosphere | 地圈；岩石圈 | Earth's solid parts, including rocks, soil, and landforms | Mountains, soil, rocks, and minerals |
| Biosphere | 生物圈 | All living organisms and the regions where life exists | Plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, and their habitats |

**How they interact:** A plant in the biosphere grows in soil from the geosphere, takes in water from the hydrosphere, and exchanges gases with the atmosphere. The spheres are different categories, but they work together as one Earth system.

**Hydrosphere versus geosphere:** The hydrosphere is Earth's water in all its forms; the geosphere is Earth's solid rock, soil, minerals, and landforms. They interact when water flows over land, erodes rock, enters soil, or becomes groundwater.

**Application example — glacier erosion:** A glacier is moving ice, so it belongs to the **hydrosphere**. The ground and rocks it scrapes, erodes, and carries as sediment belong to the **geosphere**. Therefore, the most directly involved spheres are the hydrosphere and geosphere.


**My explanation:** An ecosystem is not just a collection of organisms. It is the interaction between living organisms and the nonliving conditions that affect them.

**Important distinction — biome vs. biosphere:**

- A **biome（生物群系）** is one large climate-based region containing many related ecosystems.
- The **biosphere（生物圈）** is the whole global system of places on Earth where life exists.
- The biosphere contains all biomes. For example, deserts, grasslands, forests, tundra, and oceans are all parts of the biosphere.

**Memory aid:** A biome is one major chapter in the story of life on Earth; the biosphere is the entire book.

#### Understanding check — examples and corrections

- **Organism:** Correct. One individual human is one organism.
- **Important clarification:** “One organism” means one individual living thing, not necessarily one cell. A human, tree, or dog is one organism made of many cells; a bacterium can be one organism made of one cell.
- **Species:** Needs correction. All humans belong to one species, *Homo sapiens*. A species is a group of similar organisms, not one individual or simply “the entire human.”
- **Population:** Needs correction. A population is the members of one species living in a particular area, not the place itself. For example, the human population of Shanghai means the people living in Shanghai.
- **Community:** Partly correct. A community includes all the different populations living and interacting in an area: humans, plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms.
- **Ecosystem:** Mostly correct. An ecosystem includes all the living organisms, the nonliving environment, and their interactions. A city park can be an ecosystem.
- **Biome:** Correct example. A desert is a biome: a large climate-based region containing many related ecosystems.
- **Biosphere:** Correct. The biosphere is the global life-supporting zone containing all ecosystems and all places where life exists.

**Community versus ecosystem versus biome:** A community is the living part only—the populations of different species and their interactions. An ecosystem includes that community plus the abiotic environment, such as water, soil, air, light, and temperature. A biome is much larger: it contains many related ecosystems and is identified mainly by its climate and characteristic organisms.

**Why “community” does not require communication:** In ecology, a community means different populations living in the same area and affecting one another. These interactions do not have to involve language or intentional communication. A frog eating insects, a bee pollinating a flower, fungi decomposing a leaf, and plants competing for light are all community interactions.

**Habitat versus niche:** A habitat is where an organism lives; a niche is how it lives and what role it plays there. A frog's habitat may be a pond, while its niche includes eating insects, avoiding predators, competing for food, and serving as prey for other animals.

#### Core ideas to master

- Latitude affects temperature and precipitation. In general, lower latitudes near the equator are warmer, while higher latitudes toward the poles are cooler.
- Species richness is often higher near the equator and lower toward the poles, although this is a general pattern rather than an absolute rule.
- On land, elevation, temperature, and precipitation help determine where organisms can live.
- Elevation can also affect **solar radiation intensity**: higher locations have less atmosphere above them, so less sunlight is scattered or absorbed. Cloud cover, humidity, and the angle of sunlight also matter.
- In water, depth, light, temperature, salinity, and currents affect the distribution of aquatic life.
- **Terrestrial** means land-based; **aquatic** means water-based; **marine** refers specifically to saltwater environments.

#### Lesson 2 completion summary

**Main idea:** The distribution of life is shaped by abiotic conditions. Different regions and environments support different organisms because temperature, precipitation, sunlight, elevation, depth, salinity, and water currents vary.

**Key patterns:**

- Lower latitudes near the equator are generally warmer and often have greater species richness.
- Higher latitudes toward the poles are generally colder, so species richness often decreases.
- On land, elevation, temperature, and precipitation strongly affect where organisms can live.
- In water, depth, light, temperature, salinity, and currents affect aquatic life.
- Higher elevation usually means cooler temperatures and can also change precipitation and solar radiation intensity.
- **Species richness** means the number of different species, not the total number of individual organisms.

### Learning entry — Lesson 3 article interpretation — Limiting factors of organism distributions

**Source:** [Khan Academy — Limiting factors of organism distributions](https://www.khanacademy.org/science/hs-bio/x230b3ff252126bb6%3Aecology-and-natural-systems/x230b3ff252126bb6%3Aan-organisms-niche/a/limiting-factors-of-organism-distributions)

#### The article's central idea

Abiotic factors influence both **where an organism can be found** and **whether it can survive there**. The geographic area where an organism occurs throughout its lifetime is its **range**.

#### Two ways abiotic factors limit organisms

1. **Physical barriers:** Mountains, deserts, floods, wildfires, and volcanoes can block movement or separate suitable areas. Aquatic organisms may also be restricted to freshwater or saltwater.
2. **Survival conditions:** Temperature, light, and moisture affect metabolism, movement, growth, reproduction, and the ability to carry out life processes. A factor can be harmful when there is too little or too much of it.

#### Other important factors

- Chemical nutrients in soil, including nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium;
- salinity, or the saltiness of water;
- pH, which describes how acidic or basic an environment is;
- dissolved oxygen in water.

#### Coral example

Reef-building corals are mainly found in warm tropical and subtropical waters. Water temperature limits their range: they generally cannot survive in water below about 18°C. This illustrates the reasoning pattern:

```text
abiotic factor → effect on survival → geographic range
```

**My interpretation:** A species is not distributed randomly. Its range reflects the environmental conditions it can tolerate, the barriers it cannot cross, and the conditions that allow it to survive and reproduce.

### Learning entry — Lesson 3 article interpretation — An organism's niche

**Source:** [Khan Academy — An organism's niche](https://www.khanacademy.org/science/hs-bio/x230b3ff252126bb6%3Aecology-and-natural-systems/x230b3ff252126bb6%3Aan-organisms-niche/a/organism-niche)

#### Tolerance and conditions

Every organism needs suitable abiotic conditions to develop, survive, and reproduce. Its **tolerance** is its ability to survive variation in an abiotic factor, such as temperature, sunlight, salinity, acidity, or dissolved oxygen. Each factor has a tolerance range:

- **Optimal conditions:** the organism can survive and reproduce successfully.
- **Stressful conditions:** the organism may survive, but growth or reproduction is reduced.
- **Extreme conditions:** conditions pass the limits of tolerance, so the organism cannot survive.

#### Fundamental niche versus realized niche

- The **fundamental niche** is the full set of environmental conditions in which an organism could exist, based mainly on its abiotic tolerance ranges.
- The **realized niche** is the portion of that potential space the organism actually uses after competition, predation, and resource availability are considered.

In general, when biologists say “an organism's niche,” they usually mean its realized niche. The niche therefore includes both abiotic requirements and interactions with other organisms.

#### Specialists and generalists

- A **specialist** has a narrow niche, needs specific resources, and often has a restricted geographic range. The Kirtland's warbler, for example, nests near young jack pine trees.
- A **generalist** has a broad niche, can use many resources, and often lives in many environments. Raccoons are generalists because they can live in forests and cities and eat varied foods.

**Important interpretation:** A niche is more than an address. It describes the conditions an organism can tolerate, the resources it uses, and the relationships that determine where it actually lives.

**Life-stage example:** Caterpillars eat plant leaves, while adult butterflies usually feed on flower nectar. The same species can therefore have different resource use and interactions.

### Learning entry — Unit 4 learning log — Population growth and carrying capacity

**Learned:**

- Population density is the number of individuals per area; dispersion is their arrangement. Clumped means grouped; uniform means evenly spaced; random means unpredictable.
- Births and immigration add individuals. Deaths and emigration remove individuals.
- Exponential growth is J-shaped; logistic growth is S-shaped.
- Carrying capacity (K) is the maximum population size an environment can support. Population size can fluctuate above and below K.
- Limiting factors include resources, competition, predation, disease, parasitism, temperature, drought, floods, and fires.
- Density-dependent factors become stronger as a population becomes crowded. Density-independent factors affect populations regardless of density.
- Parasitism can lower carrying capacity; an ectoparasite lives on the outside of its host.
- r-selected and K-selected traits form a spectrum. r-selected species generally reproduce quickly with many offspring; K-selected species generally live longer, produce fewer offspring, and provide more care. Both can show exponential or logistic growth.
- Yeast is a microscopic fungus used to study population growth.

**Mistakes and corrections:**

- Immigration must be greater than emigration for movement to increase a population.
- If births and deaths are both 18 per 100 leopards, the current population count is stable. Age structure can affect future growth, but not the current count in that question.
- Carrying capacity is affected by both resource availability and limiting factors. Population size often fluctuates above and below K.

- How do carrying capacity and limiting factors change over time?
